# Professional English Listening Content System

## Product

We create short professional English listening lessons for intermediate and advanced English learners who want to understand and communicate more effectively in real professional situations.

The learners can generally read English but may struggle with:

- fast, natural professional speech
- recognising phrases in real time
- recalling what they heard
- speaking confidently in professional situations
- knowing how to respond appropriately in common workplace situations

The product uses this learning flow:

**Listen → Pause → Repeat aloud → Write what was heard → Replay/Continue → Reveal transcript → Answer one comprehension question**

The learner controls when to pause.

Writing is part of the practice method, but the learner may either type in the browser or use pen and paper.

Lessons are not traditional English lessons. They should feel like listening to a short slice of a real professional conversation.

---

## Content Purpose

Each recording should expose the learner to realistic professional English that they could hear in meetings, calls, presentations, interviews, reviews, negotiations, handovers, feedback conversations, or workplace discussions.

The goal is not only to expose learners to professional vocabulary.

A strong lesson should help the learner practise three things together:

- understanding natural spoken English
- recognising useful professional language in context
- observing how professionals handle common workplace situations

The recording should be interesting and useful enough to listen to even without the language-learning exercise.

---

## Primary Audience

The default MVP audience is junior to mid-level professionals who already have basic knowledge of their domain but want to understand and communicate more effectively in English at work.

Lessons should generally avoid requiring senior leadership experience, deep specialist expertise, or highly specialised professional judgement unless specifically requested.

The learner should normally understand the underlying work situation even if they struggle with the English used to handle it.

---

## Audience and Progression

Lessons may target different levels of professional experience and responsibility.

Do not assume job titles map cleanly to seniority. Titles such as Lead, Senior, Manager, Principal, or Head vary between companies.

Use three separate dimensions when audience targeting is needed:

### Experience

- junior
- mid
- senior

### Responsibility

- individual contributor
- lead
- manager
- stakeholder-facing

### Situation Complexity

- routine
- ambiguous
- high-stakes

Default MVP audience:

- experience: junior to mid
- responsibility: individual contributor
- complexity: routine to moderately ambiguous

Audience metadata may influence:

- the type of workplace situation
- how much ambiguity is present
- how directly people communicate
- the level of professional judgement required
- the amount of ownership expected
- the complexity of the language
- the degree of disagreement, negotiation, or stakeholder management

The purpose is not only to practise English at the learner's current professional level. Lessons may also expose learners to communication patterns commonly expected at the next level of responsibility.

Examples of progression:

### Junior → Mid

- asking better clarification questions
- giving concise status updates
- raising risks earlier
- explaining blockers clearly
- proposing a next step instead of only reporting a problem
- asking for prioritisation when tasks conflict

### Mid → Senior

- challenging assumptions
- handling ambiguity
- influencing without authority
- communicating trade-offs
- balancing competing priorities
- making recommendations under uncertainty
- disagreeing while preserving alignment

### Senior → Lead / Stakeholder-facing

- aligning multiple perspectives
- handling difficult disagreement
- communicating uncertainty clearly
- framing decisions
- managing expectations
- negotiating priorities
- explaining trade-offs to non-specialists

Do not present one communication style as universally correct. Model plausible professional approaches and sound reasoning.

---

## Lesson Length

Target spoken duration:

**2–4 minutes**

Preferred:

**2:30–3:30**

Avoid:

- under 90 seconds
- over 4 minutes

unless specifically requested.

The lesson should be short enough for intensive listening practice but long enough for a situation to develop naturally.

---

## Audience Language Levels

Default language level:

**B2**

### B1

- simpler sentence structure
- more explicit meaning
- less jargon
- fewer implied meanings
- slightly lower conversational complexity

### B2

- natural professional speech
- common domain terminology
- some idiomatic and conversational language
- disagreement, clarification, and inference are allowed
- normal professional sentence structures

### C1

- denser ideas
- subtler disagreement
- more domain terminology
- more implied meaning
- greater ambiguity
- more complex professional reasoning

Do not make speakers sound like language teachers.

Do not artificially simplify pronunciation to make the audio easier.

Language level and professional seniority are separate dimensions. A learner may be professionally senior while studying B2 English, or professionally junior while studying C1 English.

## Core Lesson Types

Lessons should focus on realistic workplace situations where the learner needs to understand what is happening and respond appropriately in English.

The goal is not only to expose learners to professional vocabulary. A good lesson should also model how professionals may handle common situations.

Prefer high-frequency situations such as:

### Good Examples

- asking a manager or colleague for clarification when a task is unclear
- pushing back on an unrealistic deadline
- explaining why work is delayed
- giving a status update when something has gone wrong
- disagreeing with a colleague without sounding unnecessarily confrontational
- responding to critical feedback
- raising a concern in a meeting
- asking someone to prioritise competing requests
- explaining a mistake and proposing a next step
- asking for help or additional context without sounding unprepared
- explaining a recommendation and answering follow-up questions
- telling a colleague or client that something cannot be done as requested
- clarifying ownership when responsibilities are unclear
- communicating a blocker before it becomes a larger problem
- negotiating a practical compromise
- handling a misunderstanding
- explaining a trade-off
- asking follow-up questions when information is incomplete

These situations are strong because they combine:

- listening comprehension
- useful professional language
- spoken response patterns
- workplace judgement
- interpersonal communication

### Weak Examples

- "A conversation about leadership"
- "Let's discuss marketing"
- "A manager talks about strategy"
- "Two colleagues discuss business"
- "Today we will learn about finance"
- conversations where both speakers simply exchange facts with no purpose
- generic discussions that could happen in any industry without meaningful context

Every lesson should contain:

- a concrete situation
- a reason for the conversation
- a clear professional context
- some form of tension, uncertainty, decision, explanation, or problem

Industry provides the **context**. The professional situation provides the **lesson**.

---

## Scenario Recipe

Every scenario should contain:

1. Domain
2. Situation
3. Roles
4. Immediate context
5. Tension, uncertainty, or unresolved issue
6. Goal
7. Outcome
8. Useful professional language

At least one of these should happen:

- disagreement
- clarification
- challenge
- negotiation
- explanation
- decision
- recommendation
- unexpected problem
- trade-off
- prioritisation
- feedback
- escalation
- correction of an assumption

Avoid conversations where both speakers simply agree and exchange information.

---

## Focus Rule

Each lesson should centre on one primary professional situation or communication problem.

Do not combine several unrelated workplace problems into one 2–4 minute lesson.

The learner should be able to answer:

**"What was this conversation mainly about?"**

The final comprehension question should normally relate to this central situation.

---

## Professional Behaviour Rule

Lessons may model useful ways of handling workplace situations, but they should not imply that there is always one universally correct professional response.

Workplace expectations vary between:

- companies
- teams
- industries
- cultures
- roles
- levels of responsibility

Prefer responses that demonstrate broadly useful behaviours such as:

- asking for clarification
- explaining reasoning
- acknowledging concerns
- proposing alternatives
- communicating uncertainty
- confirming next steps
- surfacing risks
- checking assumptions
- summarising agreements

Avoid presenting subjective workplace behaviour as a hard rule.

The lesson may demonstrate a credible way to handle a situation without claiming it is the only correct way.

---

## Dialogue Style

The dialogue must sound spoken, not written.

Use:

- short and medium-length turns
- natural reactions
- follow-up questions
- clarification
- occasional incomplete sentences
- contractions
- natural discourse markers
- professional but conversational wording
- realistic differences in perspective
- natural transitions between ideas

Examples of acceptable spoken language:

> "So what's actually driving the drop?"

> "I don't think that's the whole story."

> "Right, but if we delay now, we're going to miss the client deadline."

> "Okay. Walk me through that."

> "I'd push back on that assumption."

Avoid excessive filler such as "um", "uh", and repeated hesitation.

Use hesitation only when it contributes to realism.

Do not make every sentence perfectly formed. Real professional speech is often concise, interrupted, or partially implied.

---

## Never Write Textbook Dialogue

Avoid dialogue such as:

> "Good morning, Sarah. Today I would like to discuss our quarterly financial performance."

> "Certainly, David. Let us begin by analysing the revenue variance."

This sounds educational rather than professional.

Prefer:

> "Have you looked at the Q3 numbers yet?"

> "Yeah. That's actually what I wanted to talk about."

The learner should feel that they are listening to a real workplace interaction, not an English textbook exercise.

---

## Professional Language

Each lesson should naturally contain approximately **5–8 useful expressions, collocations, or domain terms**.

Prefer:

- phrases commonly used in meetings
- professional collocations
- domain terminology
- reusable workplace expressions
- language used to clarify, disagree, recommend, explain, prioritise, or negotiate

The scenario comes first.

Target expressions should support the scenario, not determine it.

Never distort a realistic conversation simply to include a vocabulary item.

It is acceptable for some candidate expressions not to appear if they do not fit naturally.

Do not create a vocabulary lesson inside the dialogue.

Do not say things such as:

> "The phrase 'push back' means..."

The learner should encounter professional language naturally in context.

---

## Realism Rule

Before finalising a script, ask:

**"Would professionals plausibly have this conversation at work?"**

If not, rewrite it.

Professional accuracy matters.

Do not invent unrealistic:

- procedures
- responsibilities
- organisational structures
- industry practices
- regulations
- technical facts

merely to create dialogue or include vocabulary.

If a scenario depends on specialist domain knowledge, do not invent facts, procedures, regulations, or professional practices.

Keep scenarios within common professional knowledge unless reliable domain context has been provided.

Avoid situations where one professional explains information that the other person would obviously already know purely for the learner's benefit.

---

## Engagement Rule

The first **20–30 seconds** should establish:

- what is happening
- who is involved
- what matters
- why the conversation is worth following

There should normally be an unresolved question, tension, problem, decision, or uncertainty.

The learner should want to understand what happens next.

Avoid long greetings, introductions, background exposition, or unnecessary small talk before the real situation begins.

---

## Voices and Speaker Structure

Most lessons should use two speakers because dialogue naturally exposes learners to interaction, turn-taking, clarification, and response patterns.

Use one-speaker monologues when naturally appropriate, such as:

- presenting an update
- explaining a recommendation
- summarising an incident
- giving a short professional briefing
- explaining a decision
- presenting findings

For dialogue lessons:

- speakers should have distinct roles
- speakers should have distinct perspectives when appropriate
- each speaker should have a reason to participate
- avoid making one speaker exist only to ask artificial questions for the learner

---

## Audio Realism

Audio should sound like normal professional speech rather than educational listening material.

Prefer:

- natural conversational pace
- normal contractions
- natural sentence stress
- natural connected speech
- distinct speaker voices
- realistic pauses between turns
- natural changes in emphasis
- clear but normal professional delivery

Avoid:

- exaggeratedly clear pronunciation
- unnaturally slow speech
- announcer-style delivery
- excessive emotional acting
- robotic pacing
- background music for MVP
- sound effects for MVP

Accent guidance:

Use clear, natural English appropriate to the speaker.

For MVP, prioritise comprehensibility and natural delivery.

Do not deliberately remove normal accent characteristics or connected speech.

Do not imply that one accent is the correct professional standard.

---

## Audio Preparation

The script will be converted to AI-generated speech.

Therefore:

- use clean speaker labels
- keep spoken text separate from metadata
- avoid difficult formatting
- use punctuation that supports natural speech rhythm
- avoid very long paragraphs
- avoid stage directions inside spoken text
- avoid unnatural parenthetical information
- use contractions where a real speaker normally would
- keep names easy to pronounce unless a specific name is important to the scenario

Scripts should work when read aloud without requiring major rewriting.

---

## Transcript

The transcript must exactly match the final spoken script.

Do not simplify, summarise, or rewrite the transcript after generating the audio.

If the spoken script changes before final audio generation, update the transcript.

The transcript is the learner's reference for reviewing what they heard and wrote, so accuracy is essential.

---

## Comprehension Task

Every MVP lesson has exactly **one comprehension question**.

The question should test understanding of the central meaning or outcome, not vocabulary memorisation.

Prefer questions such as:

- Why did the speaker recommend delaying the launch?
- What was the main cause of the revenue miss?
- What concern did the colleague raise?
- What did the two speakers finally agree to do?
- Why did the speaker disagree with the original plan?

Avoid questions such as:

- "What does 'revenue variance' mean?"
- "Which vocabulary word was used?"
- grammar-focused questions
- trivia about an insignificant detail

Use four multiple-choice answers.

Only one answer should clearly be correct.

Wrong answers should be plausible based on the conversation rather than obviously absurd.

The question should be answerable from listening comprehension alone.

---

## Required Lesson Output

When generating a finished lesson, the content system should support output in this order:

1. Lesson metadata
2. Scenario brief
3. Target professional language
4. Final spoken script
5. Exact transcript
6. Comprehension question
7. Correct answer
8. Explanation
9. Content quality review

The final machine-readable lesson format may later be defined separately by the lesson generation recipe or application schema.

---

## Quality Bar

Before a lesson is considered finished, review it for:

### Realism
Would professionals plausibly have this interaction?

### Spoken Naturalness
Does it sound like people speaking rather than prose being read aloud?

### Professional Accuracy
Are the roles, responsibilities, language, and situation credible?

### Audience Fit
Does the professional complexity match the intended audience?

### Language Difficulty
Does the English match the requested CEFR level?

### Engagement
Is there a reason to keep listening?

### Focus
Is there one clear central situation?

### Professional Usefulness
Does the interaction model language or behaviour the learner could plausibly use at work?

### Artificial Language
Are there any textbook-like, overly formal, repetitive, or obviously AI-generated sentences?

### Target Language Fit
Do useful expressions appear naturally rather than being forced?

### Listening Suitability
Does the script contain varied, natural speech that is worth practising intensively?

### TTS Suitability
Will the script sound natural when converted to speech?

### Assessment Quality
Does the final question test the main meaning rather than a minor detail?

Fix problems before producing the final version.

Do not merely report problems that can reasonably be corrected.

## Default Assumptions

Unless specified otherwise:

- Language level: B2
- Spoken duration: approximately 3 minutes
- Format: two-person conversation
- Audience experience: junior to mid
- Responsibility: individual contributor
- Situation complexity: routine to moderately ambiguous
- Question count: 1
- Target expressions: approximately 5–8
- Tone: realistic, professional, conversational
- Audio style: clear natural professional speech
- Background music: none
- Sound effects: none
- Transcript: exact match to final spoken script

These are defaults, not permanent restrictions.

They may later be overridden by lesson metadata as the content library expands.
